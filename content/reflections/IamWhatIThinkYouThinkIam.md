We are many things, we become many people, and we live many lives inside one life of our own. We learn, we grow, we change... We change yet not becoming the change itself, we are always becoming someone, yet rarely being the same for two days in a row. We are a plethora of contradictions. So, the question arises: *who are we really*. Who am I? What decides what I am? Is it genetics, the environment, the knowledge I gathered over the years, or something else? The answer probably is *all of these*, and that is true in a way. We are, after all, the sum total of all the things that ever happen to us. That is precisely what makes each of us unique.

However, there is an argument to be made about the influence of the *perceived judgements of others* on our development and on our very being. Emphasis on the word **“perceived.”** There have been psychological studies on children , in which some children are randomly picked from a class and told that their teachers and peers think highly of them and expect them to do well in exams. And somehow, this randomly selected group of children outperforms others, outperforms the average, and even outperforms their own previous performances, scoring better . These studies do not involve *real external judgement* because, for the most part, the teachers and other peers do not really think of these children as particularly smart or hardworking (they were being lied to that their teachers and peers do think so). It is, in fact, the very fact that they themselves believe that others think highly of them that leads them to live up to that belief. Hence, in a way, they change themselves based on their perceived judgement of others.

While its true that so much of who we are is determined by genetics, environment, upbringing, and other factors that are, for all intents and purposes, outside our control. I do not want to underplay them,but at the same time, the impact of this *“perceived judgement”* remains a huge factor influencing the innumerable facets of our nature. It pertains to the deep human desire to meet expectations, or to be what we think we ought to be. The power of this perceived judgement on us is powerful and also frightening. In the right situations, it can allow us to outgrow our limitations and become better; under the wrong influences, it can make us monsters, or at least numb us to the evil that we or others around us may possess.
> *You can tell a man that others think he is a kind man, and he may well live his life trying to be true to that label. Or you may tell a man that others think of him as a heartless killing machine, and he may spend his life being that killing machine.*

There are three plausible levels of explanation for this phenomenon. The first is the evolutionary need for acceptance into the community. Being social animals, our survival for the majority of human existence depended very much on being part of a community and thus the powerful need to conform to what others think of us. After all If they think I am good at throwing stones but I am not , then at some point , in some hunting expedition that will come to bite all of us . So it's important that become what I am expected to become . Secondly, the element of judgement is transformed into perceived judgement by the mere fact that we lack access to other people’s thoughts and opinions. Our brains are constantly trying to process social cues to find out what others think of us and, in doing so, interpret what is actually there. There are credible scientific theories suggesting that this complex task of processing social cues was a major factor in the growth and increasing complexity of the human brain compared with that of other animals. It might have been a very important factor in the emergence of consciousness.

The third and perhaps a deeper explanation has to do with the question of why we want to become aligned with this perceived judgement. To me, the answer is the fundamental human desire for meaning. A human finds himself gaining consciousness in a piece of flesh, and then the conscious selves demand meaning from our existence. While our genetic and external factors determine our physical traits and social behaviours, the question of “Who am I, and why am I?” remains unresolved in the subconscious. And since, for most or all of us, there exists no satisfactory answer to this question, perceived judgements come in to fill the gap and tell us what we are and what we ought to be. In a way, it is the lack of personal meaning that makes us so prone to perceived external labels. We , for most part , do not know who we are or why we are , so naturally we look outwards and try to decipher the answer to these questions through others .

While I am in no way capable of judging whether this process of internalising perceived judgements is good or bad, or what to turn to for personal meaning in the absence of others ( *althoughit raises the bigger question of whether consciousness can exist in a vacuum, in the absence of others or not*). I can say this: it is afterall a perceived judgement, a reflection of us in the constructed mental imagery of others. Remembering that it says much more about us than about others helps us not feel like pawns and gives us back power over our own selves.We cast the light of who we believe ourselves to be upon others, and that light returns to us through their reflections. They are the mirrors; the light, however, remains our own.We can change the image reflected back to us by changing the light we cast. We are not prisoners of what we believe others think of us. For even if we cannot change their thoughts, we can change our perception of them. And in changing what we believe they see in us, we may ultimately change what we see in ourselves.

> *Others are mirrors through which you see yourself .When you clean the mirror, the reflection you see of yourself becomes clearer*

You cannot change what others think of you, but you can change what you think of others.When you humanise them and construct a positive image of them in your mind, you begin to clean the very mirror through which you see yourself. When you choose to think of others with love, you begin to change not only how you see them, but also how you see yourself through them.Recognise that they, too, are products of the unforgiving currents of time and circumstances. Their thoughts are imperfect, their judgments are flawed, and they themselves are far from perfect. Yet beneath all their imperfections, you can still choose to see the innocent child within them, the human spirit that exists beneath the layers of experience, fear, and conditioning. You can choose to show them love. You can choose to think highly of them, not because they are without flaws but because you recognise their humanity despite those flaws.

And perhaps, when you begin to see others through this lens of love, the mirror slowly clears. The judgment you once feared begins to lose its weight, and the image you perceive of yourself through the eyes of others also becomes gentler, kinder, and more forgiving.You may never know what others truly think of you. You may never be able to change their judgments. But you can choose the spirit in which you perceive them. Choose a kind spirit.And the love you show to others would find its ways back to you.
