Is it bad to slap someone, lie, or steal? Most of us would agree that these actions are *bad*, *wrong*, or *immoral*. But what does it mean to be moral, to do what is right, or to be a good person? Morality has been a fundamental question throughout human history and may have emerged when the first conscious humans began interacting with one another. Religion has played a critical role in defining ethical principles, though I would argue that the relationship between religion and morality could easily be reversed: morality may have existed first and given rise to religion—or at least to the need for religion and laws. Morality also lies at the centre of questions about consciousness and the cosmological arguments discussed in the earlier essays. Despite the efforts of countless thinkers, intellectuals, and scientists, morality remains an open question.

However, in contemporary times, two philosophers have managed to propose promising yet contradictory theories about the morality: David Hume, with his *empiricism* — the view that all knowledge is based on experience — and Emmanual Kant, with *noumena* and the *categorical imperative*.

Hume argues that all morality is based on sentiments. He wrote that *“Reason is, and ought to be, the slave of the passions.”* All reason is , is merely a vessel for desires, and moral judgment comes primarily from human feelings and sympathy. To Hume, stealing is immoral because it leads to feelings of disgust and sympathy for the person or victim who is stolen from. It is the collective reaction or emotion that a particular action arouses that decides the morality (or goodness/badness) of an action. We see this in action all the time: ask people what they think about killing insects, and you would very easily find that the moral judgment is skewed and differs significanlty for killing a butterfly or a cockroach, or for killing a cat and a sheep. When you witness this, you cannot help but wonder if Hume was right and moral judgment is merely an emotional reaction dressed up as reason. Hume argues that reason is itself a means for desire; it does not tell us what to want, but rather devises how to get what we desire.

> *There are no moral phenomena at all, but only a moral interpretation of phenomena.*

> *If ugliness dims the light of our compassion,*  
> *and beauty makes the heart more willing to care,*  
> *is morality forged in reason’s quiet fire—*  
> *or shaped by the feelings we carry there?*

Kant was deeply troubled by this theory of Hume when he ecoountered it during his studies in Germany. Hume shook Kant and challenged his entire worldview . He spent years trying to refute it, since it dealt a huge blow to Kant’s personal and religious beliefs. It somehow subjectified morality, the distinction between right and wrong became a smatter of feelings and in the absence of an objective morality, what role does religion possibly play but to be a tool for the desires, as Hume presumed?

Kant spent years in trouble and trying to come up with a coherent reply. This was the time in history when Newton was revolutionizing the world of science by discovering fundamental physical laws, and Kant wondered if such laws could be formulated in metaphysics as well , moral laws that are true across cultures, space, and time. He ultimately came up with the theory that is perhaps best described by the line *“the only good thing is a good will”*—an inherently Abrahamic idea that actions are defined by their intention. Kant’s main idea was the categorical imperative: *“Act only according to a maxim that you could consistently will to become a universal law.”*


It is essentially the centuries-old rule: do unto others what you would wish to see common. A Mafia boss who kills people would never want a world in which killing is the norm; he himself would hate to be killed when coming back from his murder assignment. He knows that the maxim should not be a universal law, yet he draws an exception for himself, and according to Kant, this process of carving out an exception makes the act immoral. The same could be said for tax returns. We don’t want everyone to lie on their tax returns, but we ourselves wouldn’t mind lying to save money—wishing an exception to this rule and thus rendering the act immoral.

> *What is not good for the swarm, is not good for the bee*

Another distinction between Hume’s and Kant’s theories, as discussed earlier, is “will” or “intention.” Consider two cases. In Case A, someone sitting by a window intentionally drops his laptop to kill a person below. In Case B, the laptop is dropped by accident and unintentionally falls on someone’s head, killing him. According to Hume’s framework, these two cases are almost the same and equally immoral, since both create disgust or disapproval and sympathy for the victim (you might argue that knowing Case B was accidental slightly reduces disgust, but not by much). For Kant, however, the two cases are completely different. The person in Case B had no intention to kill, so he is morally blameless.

Perhaps one interesting aspect is the *categorical imperative* of Kant and the *hypothetical imperative* of Hume. For Hume, desires set the end, and reason or action is the means to that end. It essentially says: *“Do X if you want Y.”* The categorical imperative, on the other hand, establishes fundamental duties of all rational actors and lays out the rule of *“Do X because it is right.”* It is something you must do because it is morally right and you are a rational being.

Trying to judge between these two theories is like playing quantum mechanics with morality — the more strongly you try to ascertain the truth, the less confident you get. We saw the examples with insects demonstrating the obvious virtue of Hume’s theory. Now consider this: I think we all learn and teach Kantian morality all the time (knowingly or not) , we teach it to every child in kindergarten. Let’s say Ali and Fatima are six years old and playing. Ali, being stronger, seizes Fatima’s doll. Now every adult would tell Ali that it is wrong. It is wrong because what if everyone starts using force to seize others’ toys? Hamza is stronger, and what if he seizes your toy — would you like it Ali? Probably not. (Act only according to a maxim that you could consistently will to become a universal law) . We just taught and applied Kant’s morality to a six-year-old, and we do this all the time. Our rules and constitutions are primarily based on the categorical imperative and the concept of “will”. It can be because we really do believe Kant’s theory, or it might be that we follow Kant’s rational theory to *feel* reasonable and feel good about ourselves (Hume’s wink) while our desires drive our real decisions.How many times do we see the champions of morality lowering their logic when brown kids get killed in Gaza instead of in Europe, or the thousand rational legal loopholes for the rich and pretty? (Again, Hume’s wink.)
