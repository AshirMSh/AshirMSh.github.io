A question that has gripped each and every thinking human brain is the question about the existence of the universe and the existence of God. Today, we find plenty of people who are uncertain, some still searching for an answer to this rather impossibly hard to settle question, and others who have given up and become atheist/agnostics. There is a third group, which perhaps still forms the majority of the world’s population, that turns towards organized religion to satisfy their need for an answer. This question, as old as human existence itself, is no small feat to answer.I am not going to try to answer this question by myself. I am a Muslim and I do have my own beliefs, but today I want to discuss arguments/philosophies pertinent to this ontological question. We are going to list and try to understand the Cosmological Arguments, i.e. the arguments in favor of an  existence external to the universe. These rational arguments try to establish and prove that the existence of the universe cannot be substantially explained by any cause within it; thus, there must exist an external cause  - “God.”

I shall present three types of these cosmological arguments: *Kalam cosmological argument, Aquinas' cosmological argument, and Leibniz' contingency argument*. Though there may exist more, I shall try to focus on these and explain my understanding of the arguments and their limitations.


The first and the oldest of the three is the Kalam Argument. As the name suggests, it finds its origins in the Muslim tradition. Ibn Sina was amongst its earlier proponent, and in modern times William Lain Craig has reinvigorated it (you can even find his debates on YouTube). The strength of the argument perhaps lies in its simplicity. It draws on the principle of **causality**, i.e. *everything that exists has a beginning and a cause; the universe exists, thus it must have a beginning and a cause; this cause is an existence that precedes the universe and is God*. There is an inherent assumption that the principle of causality and birth holds outside of the physical universe as well. This seems to be the logical line of reasoning, and Occam’s Razor supports it as well. The scientific discoveries surrounding big bang in the last century granted some material weight to be argument , though like all arguments it remains a philosophical argument not a scientific one. The argument esatblishes a *'first cause / first move'* through which universe came into being , however I wonder how it further develops that into existence of a personal God (one who is omni-present and aware of your thaughts). *(I shall come back to this point in the future essays)*.

> *Thus in a way **consciousness** is *'to be'*, while **intelligence** is the ability *'to do'*.”*

The second argument is widely popularizd by Thomas Aquinas . At first, it may be easy to confuse it with the Kalam argument, but the difference is subtle yet significant. Instead of reasoning in the temporal dimension (which raises many questions about the nature of time), it tries to reason along the spatial dependency dimension , in terms of dependency and causation. Consider this: I am holding a pen; the pen depends on my hand;my hand depends on my arm; I depend on the ground/chair where I sit; the ground depends on Earth’s structure, and so on.Each element in this chain depends on something else for its existence or activity. By following this reasoning, we must approach a cause that is not dependent on any other cause (assumes that an infinite regression of causes is not possible); thus, at this very moment, there must exist a *“first cause”* or *"unmoved mover"* that is continuously sustaining everything. It does not explicitly depend on time and does not require the first cause to be the first event *in time*,but claims that a non-dependent clause must exists here and now.  Nevertheless , when the question of the birth of the universe is concerned, its response is rather similar to that of Kalam.

The third and perhaps a bit different argument comes from Leibniz. Consider any object near you; let’s take this pen. There is nothing logically necessary about its existence : the pen could have failed to exist and is therefore *contingent* .It might not have existed (contingent existence), but the very fact that it does exist precludes that there must be some sufficient reason why it exists rather than not.It was spawned into existence by a willing entity, willing and able. Leibniz asked the question **what explains the existence of everything that is contingent**. We can explain everything within universe in terms of someting else within the universe ,but we have not yet explained why there is a universe in the first place. The ultimate explanation cannot itselft be another contingent thing creating a loop. Thus there must be something who existence is *necessary* (non-contingent) , that does not depend on anything else for its existence and provides sufficient reason for existence of everything else . Leibniz identifies this necessary being with God.

One aspect that you might have already picked is that these arguments (particularly Kalam and Aquinas) have an implicit assumption that “infinity” cannot exist in the realm of reality. They establish that one cannot have infinite regression of causes in temporal or spatial sense; thus, there must be a *“first mover”* or an *"unmoved mover"* .Atheists raise their concern on this assumption on the basis of 3 arguments: 1) How can we be certain that infinity doesn’t exist? 2) If this first cause exists beyond space and time, then wouldn’t its existence preclude its infinity? 3) Even if there was a first cause, how does it qualify as the modern, Abrahamic God? The last two concerns apply to Leibniz as well. I shall try to come back and address these questions in a future essay.

To summarize, these arguments establish the first cause by rejecting infinity. Kalam rejects infinite time; Aquinas rejects infinite causal regression; and Leibniz plays on contingency (infinite possibilities). Yet perhaps the most profound inquiry lies not in these arguments themselves, but in the nature of infinity — whether it exists as a feature of reality or merely as a conceptual abstraction. For those who ground their understanding in empirical observation and the parsimony of Occam's Razor, infinity becomes not a principle of nature but a logical impossibility; and in the absence of such infinite regress, these cosmological arguments emerge as compelling testimonies to the existence of a transcendent cause beyond the temporal and spatial constraints of our universe , proving existence of God . Billions of people believe in them, knowingly or unknowingly, and no one has yet been able to conclusively refute them.
